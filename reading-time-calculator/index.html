How the estimates work

These are estimates, not exact predictions. Reading time is based on the speed you choose above: Slow is about 150 words per minute, Normal is about 200 words per minute, and Fast is about 250 words per minute. Speaking time is always based on about 130 words per minute, which is a normal speaking speed. Empty text gives 0 minutes. Real times vary with the text, the topic and the reader — for example, a 1,000-word article takes about 5 minutes to read at Normal speed and about 7–8 minutes to speak aloud. Very short texts show less than 1 minute, which is correct but not very useful — the estimates are most helpful for texts of a few hundred words or more.

How to use this reading time calculator

This reading time calculator estimates how long your text takes to read or to speak aloud. Paste your text into the box above and the estimates update live. The total word count is shown at the top, because the estimates are based on that count. There is no sign-up, and your text stays on your device.

Use the reading time when you want to know if an article, a lesson or a set of notes is the right length. Use the speaking time when you are planning a talk, a lesson, a presentation or a video script. If the speaking time is longer than the time you have, you need to cut the text or speak faster — and cutting the text is usually the better choice.

How the reading time is estimated

The reading time is an estimate based on about 200 words per minute. That is a normal reading speed for most adults reading ordinary text. The speaking time is based on about 130 words per minute, which is a normal speaking speed. These are estimates, not exact predictions. Real times vary with the text, the topic and the reader.

A simple text with short words is faster to read than a difficult text with long words and long sentences. A reader who knows the topic well will read faster than a reader who is learning it for the first time. A speaker who pauses for effect, or who waits for questions, will take longer than the estimate. Use the numbers as a guide, and add a little extra time if the text is difficult or if you plan to pause.

As a rough guide, 500 words takes about 2 to 3 minutes to read and about 4 minutes to speak aloud. 1,000 words takes about 5 minutes to read and about 7 to 8 minutes to speak aloud. 2,000 words takes about 10 minutes to read and about 15 minutes to speak aloud. These are the same speeds the calculator uses, worked out for common lengths.

Speaking time calculator: planning a talk

A speaking time calculator is useful when you have a fixed time for a talk. If you have 10 minutes, plan for about 1,300 words at 130 words per minute. That leaves a little time for pauses and for a short introduction. If you write 2,000 words for a 10-minute talk, you will have to rush, and the audience will find it hard to follow.

Write your talk in the words you would actually say, not in the words you would write in an essay. Spoken sentences are usually shorter, and spoken language uses simpler words. Then paste the talk into the calculator and check the speaking time. Read it aloud once with a timer if you can. That test will tell you more than the estimate alone, because your own speaking speed may be faster or slower than 130 words per minute.

For a lesson or a video, the same idea applies. Plan the main points first, then write the script, then check the time. If the script is too long, cut a whole point rather than speaking faster. A clear talk that finishes on time is better than a rushed talk that tries to cover too much.

Tips and limits

Do not use the reading time for a single sentence or a very short text. The estimate is most useful for texts of a few hundred words or more. For short texts, the time is less than a minute, and the difference between reading and speaking is small. Also, do not treat the estimate as a promise. If you need an exact time, read the text aloud with a timer. The calculator gives you a quick plan; the timer gives you the real time.

Where the 238 words per minute figure comes from

The Research preset, 238 words per minute, comes from Brysbaert (2019), “How many words do we read per minute? A review and meta-analysis of reading rate”, published in the Journal of Memory and Language. Brysbaert reviewed published studies of silent reading in English and found an average of about 238 words per minute for non-fiction and about 260 words per minute for fiction. That is an average across many readers and many texts, not a speed every reader should aim for, and it does not mean a difficult text reads at 238 words per minute — a dense textbook chapter will be slower, and a light novel will often be faster.

That is why this page offers four speeds instead of one. Slow (150) fits a difficult text, a text in a second language, or careful study reading, where you stop to think. Normal (200) is a round, easy-to-remember middle speed for ordinary articles. Fast (250) fits easy, familiar material. Research (238) is there for readers who want the published average rather than a round number. If you know your own speed — for example, you timed yourself reading a 1,000-word article in 4 minutes, which is 250 words per minute — type it in the custom speed box and the calculator will use that instead.

Worked examples

Example 1 — a 650-word essay. At Normal speed (200 words per minute), 650 ÷ 200 = 3.25 minutes, which is 3 minutes 15 seconds to read. At Slow speed it is 650 ÷ 150 = 4 minutes 20 seconds, and to speak aloud at 130 words per minute it is 650 ÷ 130 = 5 minutes exactly. So the same essay takes between about 3 minutes and 5 minutes depending on what you do with it — a useful range to quote when someone asks how long it is.

Example 2 — a 10-minute talk. A talk is planned backwards from the time, not forwards from the words. Ten minutes at 130 words per minute is 10 × 130 = 1,300 words. If your script is 1,300 words, the calculator will show a speaking time of 10 minutes. If your script is 2,000 words, it will show 15 minutes 23 seconds, which means you must cut about 700 words or accept running over. The calculator shows minutes and seconds, not whole minutes only, so a script of 1,450 words shows 11 minutes 9 seconds of speaking time — enough to see that it is over by a minute, not just “about 11 minutes”.

Example 3 — pages instead of words. Suppose you have a 4-page handout and no word count. Type 4 in the page-count box. The calculator uses about 250 words per double-spaced page, so 4 pages is about 1,000 words, which is 5 minutes to read at Normal speed and 7 minutes 42 seconds to speak. If your pages are single-spaced, they hold about 500 words each instead, so type 8 in the page-count box (or type 2,000 in the word-count box) to get the same answer by the other route.

Example 4 — a short text. A 90-word announcement at Normal speed is 90 ÷ 200 = 0.45 minutes, which is 27 seconds to read and 42 seconds to speak (90 ÷ 130 = 0.69 minutes). The calculator shows seconds for short texts instead of rounding to “0 minutes” or “less than 1 minute”, because 27 seconds is a real answer and “less than 1 minute” is not.

Reading speed by type of text

Speed depends on the text more than on the reader. A news article, a story, or a set of simple instructions usually reads near the Normal or Fast speed. A textbook, a legal document, or a text full of numbers and names usually reads near the Slow speed, because you stop, re-read, and check details. Poetry and dialogue read slowly even though the words are short, because the meaning sits between the lines. None of this is a fault in the reader — it is the text asking for more time. When you plan, pick the speed that matches the text, not the speed you wish you read at, and add a minute or two for questions or pauses if other people are listening.
